The Lorenz Attractor

The Background

In the year 1961, something happened and it happened by mistake. Lorenz was a meteorologist working at MIT. The computers then were nothing like what we have today. To simulate fluids at all, one had to simplify the math drastically. The Navier-Stokes equations are the gold standard, and we can simulate them today, but in 1961 that was a pipe dream. One day, Lorenz and his team wanted to re-run a simulation. The computer printed values of the simulation to a physical tape. In the computer, the values had six decimal places, but the values printed to the tape had only three. In most physical systems, 0.0001 makes no difference. Lorenz, on accident, was about to discover something. He restarted the simulation using the tape's values with only three decimal points, and presumably walked away to get a coffee. When he came back the computer was printing values on the tape that looked nothing like the prior simulation. This was puzzling, but Lorenz was smart and eager to figure out what was happening. He worked it out. He'd accidentally discovered a chaotic system.

What is a Chaotic System?

A chaotic system is one where the system as a whole is incredibly sensitive to initial conditions. Examples include the double pendulum, and rabbit populations. If a parameter is tweaked by 0.0001, after some time, the system will look nothing like the former system. Here's an analogy based in real life: Two childhood friends grow up in the same neighborhood. On a global scale, that's like a 0.0001 or smaller difference. They go to the same school, and constantly hang out. They should be pretty similar, but after sometime, one's living in Europe with his wife and kids and the other is touring with a Metal band as their sound guy. Life is chaotic. The chance encounters, the calculated choices, the little 0.000000001 differences in their lives causes them to drift into very different lives eventually even though they both started living on the same block, going to the same school, doing largely the same stuff.

What You're About to See

When Lorenz worked out what he'd seen in the lab, he speculated: "Could a butterfly flapping its wings in South America cause a tornado in Texas?" The math? It says yes.

This simulation recreates Lorenz's original 0.0001 rounding error that resulted in the accidental discovery. There are two paths we'll follow, a cyan and a magenta path. At first they will look identical. Eventually they won't.
